Overview

Project Ingress is an attempt to detect an exoplanet transit using a commercially available telescope. The goal is to measure how far a consumer-grade instrument can be pushed before it hits its limit of precision, and to prove it by actually catching a transit.

This page compiles every predicted exoplanet transit observable from Campbell River, British Columbia, cross-referenced against the NASA Exoplanet Archive and generated using the Swarthmore College Transit/Eclipse Observability tool (Tapir). It began as a printed reference booklet; this page mirrors that same data interactively, so the two big tables can be sorted however you need, and any event can be opened for full detail with one click.

Predicted times carry inherent uncertainty from each system's published orbital ephemeris. That uncertainty compounds with the number of orbits between now and the predicted date, so an event next week is far more reliable than one predicted months out. Always re-check a date shortly before relying on it.

Underlying event data locked at time of search: September 1, 2026. Only the "Soonest Upcoming Event" box below updates live, using your device's current date; everything else reflects that fixed search.

Ranking method

Each event is ranked by an estimated detection margin: how many multiples of the expected photometric noise floor the transit depth represents on a 30mm optical system. A 10 second, in-focus exposure of a V=7.7 star was measured to collect roughly 26,000 electrons on the sensor. Scaling that to any other magnitude, and binning across a fixed 5 minute window, gives an expected precision at that magnitude. The margin is simply the transit depth divided by that expected precision.

M(V, d) = d × 883.2 × 10−0.2(V − 7.7)

V is the host star's apparent magnitude, d is the transit depth as a decimal fraction, M is the resulting margin. Ties are broken first by percent of transit plus baseline observable, then by moon illumination.

HD 189733 b margin 21.2× rank 1

HD 189733 is one of the brightest stars known to host a transiting hot Jupiter, and it doubles as its own variable star, cataloged as V452 Vulpeculae because starspots slowly modulate its brightness as the star rotates. Its planet, HD 189733 b, was discovered in 2005 and completes an orbit in just over two days, producing a deep, easily measured 2.4 percent dip in brightness. Because the star is so bright and the transit so deep, this system has become a standard first target for both professional atmospheric studies and amateur transit photometry.

WASP-33 b margin 9.0× rank 8

WASP-33, also cataloged as HD 15082, is an A-type star spinning so quickly that its own light varies in brightness independent of its planet, making it the first star ever confirmed to be both a delta Scuti pulsating variable and an exoplanet host. Its planet, WASP-33 b, orbits in just 1.22 days on a retrograde, tilted path relative to the star's own spin. Because the star's pulsations ripple through the light curve, this target is scientifically interesting but genuinely more complex to analyze than most, since part of what shows up in the data is starlight variability, not just the planet.

HD 209458 b margin 13.2× rank 6

HD 209458 holds a permanent place in the history of exoplanet science. In 1999 it became the first star ever observed to have a planet transit in front of it, and its planet, nicknamed Osiris, went on to become the first exoplanet confirmed to have an atmosphere at all. The planet is only slightly less massive than Jupiter but noticeably larger, giving it a low density consistent with a puffed up, highly irradiated gas giant. Its brightness, magnitude 7.7, and its 1.5 percent transit depth make it a very approachable target for a modest amateur telescope.

KELT-20 b margin 14.5× rank 4

KELT-20 is a hot, fast spinning A-type star that hosts one of the most extreme planets in this catalog. KELT-20 b is classified as an ultra-hot Jupiter, meaning its dayside temperature is pushed to roughly 2300 Kelvin by the intense radiation of its host star. The star itself is quite young, only about 58 million years old, and is among the brightest known hosts of a transiting giant planet. Astronomers have already detected sodium, iron, and other metals directly in the planet's atmosphere using this same transit method.

KELT-4 A b margin 3.6× rank 97

KELT-4 A is the brightest known host star of a hot Jupiter that sits inside a hierarchical triple star system, meaning it has two additional stellar companions, KELT-4 B and C, orbiting the system as a close pair further out. The planet, KELT-4 A b, is a typical hot Jupiter on a three day orbit, discovered in 2016. Because two extra stars share the field of view, careful comparison star selection matters even more than usual when measuring this system's transit.

HAT-P-22 b margin 4.8× rank 49

HAT-P-22 is a G-type dwarf star very close in character to the Sun, located in the constellation Ursa Major, with a faint red dwarf companion orbiting far out in the same system. Its planet, HAT-P-22 b, is a genuinely massive hot Jupiter, over twice the mass of Jupiter itself, discovered in 2010. The host star has the lowest mass of any star in its original discovery survey's comparison sample, making this a useful benchmark system for studying how a lower mass star supports a heavy planet in a tight orbit.

KELT-17 b margin 4.0× rank 90

KELT-17 is an unusual host star known as an Am star, short for metallic-line A star, a class of chemically peculiar star whose atmosphere shows an overabundance of certain heavy elements like titanium, chromium, and iron. It was the first exoplanet host ever confirmed to belong to this specific chemical class. Its planet, KELT-17 b, orbits in just over three days and was discovered in 2016 using Doppler tomography, a technique that measures how tilted a planet's orbit is relative to its star's spin.

TOI-1518 b margin 4.8× rank 68

TOI-1518 is a hot F0-type star discovered by NASA's TESS mission to host one of the more extreme ultra-hot Jupiters in this catalog. TOI-1518 b orbits in under two days and is inflated to nearly twice the radius of Jupiter, with iron directly detected in its atmosphere, a rare and notable find that makes it a strong candidate for future spectroscopy aimed at detecting a thermal inversion layer. At magnitude 9.0, it sits at the fainter end of what this project's search criteria allow, but its very deep transit helps compensate.

K2-232 b margin 3.3× rank 125

K2-232 is a late F-type star observed by NASA's K2 mission, and its planet, K2-232 b, stands out for its unusually elongated, eccentric orbit rather than the nearly circular path most hot Jupiters follow. Classified as a warm Saturn, the planet takes over eleven days to complete one orbit, considerably longer than most targets in this catalog, and is one of only a handful of well characterized warm giant planets with a host star brighter than tenth magnitude. Its long period means suitable transit windows appear far less often than for the tighter orbiting hot Jupiters elsewhere in this list.

HAT-P-1 b margin 4.8× rank 51

HAT-P-1 is one half of a wide visual binary system, paired with an almost identical Sun-like star roughly 1500 astronomical units away, a companion close enough in brightness and color that astronomers have used it as a built in comparison star for unusually precise differential photometry. Its planet, HAT-P-1 b, was discovered in 2006 and is notably large and low density for its mass, a puzzle that helped motivate later theoretical models of how irradiation inflates close-in giant planets. The wide binary companion is a genuine practical advantage for amateur observers too, since a bright, reliable comparison star sits right in the same field.

Glossary

Ingress
The beginning of a transit, when the planet first begins to cross in front of the star.
Egress
The end of a transit, when the planet finishes crossing the star's disk.
Mid-transit
The moment the planet is at the center of its crossing, when brightness is lowest.
Transit depth
The fraction of starlight blocked at mid-transit, in parts per thousand (ppt) or percent.
Baseline
Observation time before ingress and after egress, used to establish the star's normal brightness.
BJD_TDB − 2,450,000
Barycentric Julian Date with 2,450,000 subtracted to keep figures short. Add it back for the full date.
Elevation / Airmass
Elevation is the star's angle above the horizon. Airmass (1/sin of elevation) measures how much atmosphere its light passes through; higher airmass means more distortion and noise.
Nautical twilight
The point at which the Sun is 12° below the horizon, used as this project's definition of usable darkness.
V magnitude
Apparent brightness as seen from Earth. Lower numbers are brighter; each step of 1 is about 2.5× dimmer.
Detection margin
How many multiples of the expected photometric noise floor a transit's depth represents on this project's instrument. See the Method section above for the formula.
Hot Jupiter / Ultra-hot Jupiter
A gas giant orbiting very close to its star, with short periods and high temperatures. "Ultra-hot" hosts are typically hot A or F-type stars exceeding ~2200K on the dayside.
Am star
Short for metallic-line A star: a chemically peculiar A-type star with an excess of certain heavy elements in its atmosphere.
Delta Scuti variable
A class of pulsating star that brightens and dims on a timescale of hours from internal oscillation, unrelated to any orbiting planet.
