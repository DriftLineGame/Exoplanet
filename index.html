Every consumer telescope has a ceiling nobody's actually measured.

Project Ingress is an attempt to detect an exoplanet transit using a commercially available telescope.

If a sub-$1,000 telescope can catch a planet crossing a star 64 light-years away, that changes what amateur astronomers are able to contribute to real exoplanet research.

What happens when you push a small telescope past what it's supposed to do?

Push past what it's "supposed" to do, and you find out what it's actually capable of, and what that capability could mean once put in enough hands.

When a planet crosses in front of its star, the star dims by roughly 0.6 to 2.4 percent for a few hours. Catching that dip takes careful, repeatable measurement, not a big telescope. That is what we are testing.

  1. Prove the process

    We will start with variable stars that have known, published brightness curves. Using a 30mm optical system, we reproduce one of these curves from our own data. A match confirms the process works.

  2. Point it at a planet

    From there, we point the same process at an exoplanet, aiming to catch a transit our team hasn't measured before.

  3. Document the ceiling

    The result we're after: a detected transit, and a documented answer to exactly how much a small telescope can do when it's actually pushed.

Our top target, found in our own photo

HD 189733 ranks first in our catalog: a bright star with a large planet that blocks 2.4% of its light every 2.2 days. It sits 15.5 arcminutes from the Dumbbell Nebula. This image was stacked from frames taken during our first transit attempt on it.

We plate-solved the photo, matching its stars to the Hipparcos catalog to work out the exact sky position of every pixel. Then we measured where HD 189733 actually is.

Progress

Every session goes in this log, including the ones the weather took.

  1. Sept 1, 2026

    Catalog complete Done

    126 predicted transits of 12 planets observable from Campbell River, ranked by how clearly our telescope should see each one. Printed as a 76-page field reference.

  2. Sept 6, 2026

    TOI-1518 b Clouded out

    Set up for the transit, but cloud covered the sky. No data.

  3. Sept 12–13, 2026

    HD 189733 b, rank #1 Photometry in progress

    Imaged from 10:26 PM to 2:20 AM with the Seestar S30 Pro in 10-second exposures. The first 24 frames were lost because saving individual frames was switched off, a lesson now built into our checklist. The frames are being processed in HOPS to build the light curve.

  4. Nov 19, 2026

    Plate solve and proper motion Done

    Located HD 189733 in the Sept 12 stack and measured its motion since 2000, agreeing with Gaia to 0.2″. Released the transit planner with a live predictor that reproduces all 126 catalog events.

  5. Next

    Finish the first light curve Up next

    Complete the HOPS photometry of the Sept 12 frames and run the variable star calibration.

    The instrument

    Everything here is done with a smart telescope anyone can buy. The numbers below are measured from our own data, not taken from a spec sheet.

    Telescope
    ZWO Seestar S30 Pro, 30 mm aperture, 160 mm focal length
    Image scale
    3.67″ per pixel, from our plate solve
    Signal
    About 26,000 electrons in 10 seconds from a magnitude 7.7 star
    Expected precision
    ±1.1 parts per thousand per 5 minutes on HD 189733, about 1/21 of its transit depth
    Exposure
    10 seconds, UV/IR-cut filter, every frame saved
    Analysis
    Differential photometry in HOPS

    Who we are

    Project Ingress is run by the Campbell River Astronomy Club Research Team, the research arm of the Campbell River Astronomy Club in Campbell River, British Columbia.

    The club runs a free membership program. Its goal is to break the financial barriers to astronomy, so anyone curious about the sky can take part.
